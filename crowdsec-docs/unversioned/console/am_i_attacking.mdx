## Where to find it

Am I Attacking lives in the **IP Reputation / CTI** section of the Console, at [app.crowdsec.net/am-i-attacking](https://app.crowdsec.net/am-i-attacking). It is a Premium feature: a Community organization sees what it unlocks and a button to the [pricing page](https://app.crowdsec.net/pricing), with a 30-day free trial.

## Declare the IPs to monitor

Click **Configurations** in the page header, then **Add a configuration**. A configuration carries a name, the addresses to watch and a deduplication window.

### IPs and CIDR ranges

Type an address or a range and press **Enter**: each entry becomes a chip. Pasting a whole list works too, whatever separates the entries. When you focus the field, the IPs of the Security Engines you run are suggested, one click away.

Entries are checked as you type, and the Console says why one is refused:

| Refused | Why |
|---|---|
| Not a valid IP or CIDR range | Only addresses and ranges, IPv4 or IPv6, can be monitored. |
| Private or reserved | Loopback, link-local, multicast, the private blocks (RFC 1918, 6598) and the documentation blocks (RFC 5737, 3849) have no owner to alert. A range overlapping one of them is refused as a whole. |
| A /16 or wider (a /112 in IPv6) | Monitoring a range that large goes through our sales team: a *Contact us* link appears under the field. |

### How many IPs a range counts for

As soon as the configuration holds a range, a summary tells you how many addresses it monitors in all: a /24 counts for 256, a /32 for one. Each address is counted once. A single IP already inside one of your ranges, or a range nested in a wider one, is marked *already inside* and adds nothing. IPv4 and IPv6 addresses count alike.

Below the summary, a notice shows how many of your organization's monitored IPs this configuration will use after saving, against your [quota](#monitored-ip-quota).

### Deduplication window

After an alert on one of your IPs, further detections on **that same IP** stay silent for the length of the window. Pick a preset (15 minutes, 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours), or choose **Custom** and set days, hours and minutes. Set every unit to zero to be notified on every detection. The default is one hour.

### Declaration of authorization

Before saving, tick the declaration that you are authorized to monitor these addresses. CrowdSec records it with your account, the addresses, the wording you agreed to and the date. It is asked **every time addresses are added**, on creation and on edition. Renaming a configuration, changing its window or removing addresses asks for nothing.

## Monitored IP quota

A Premium plan includes **3 monitored IPs**, counted across all your configurations. The gauge in the page header shows how many are used and how many are left. The same gauge sits on the **Plan** page of the organization settings, under *Am I Attacking monitored IPs*.

### Adding monitored IP slots

When a configuration would exceed the limit, the modal says by how many addresses and quotes the monthly price of the missing slots. The button becomes **Buy N slots and create configuration**: the slots are added to the subscription first, then the configuration is saved. Expect a few seconds between the two while the new limit propagates. If it takes longer, the slots are bought and the Console asks you to save again in a minute.

Only an **organization owner**, on an **active Premium subscription**, can buy slots. Editors see the shortfall and who can fix it. Slots can also be added or removed from the Plan page: an increase applies immediately, prorated over the current billing period. A decrease is scheduled for the end of the billing period, and refused while more addresses are monitored than the new limit would allow.

A Premium subscription can monitor up to **16,384 addresses** in all, the equivalent of a /18. Beyond that, the modal invites you to contact us.

:::warning During the free trial
Monitoring more than **16 IPs** is not covered by the free trial. Buying slots past that number ends the trial at once, and billing starts immediately. The modal warns you before you confirm. See the [pricing page](https://app.crowdsec.net/pricing) for the slot prices.
:::

## Get notified

Events reach you through your [notification rules](/u/console/notification_integrations/rule). Create a rule in the **Threat Hunting** category, select the **Am I Attacking** event, and pick any destination: Email, Slack, Discord or a webhook. The deduplication window of each configuration applies to every destination.

As long as no rule carries the event and reaches an active integration, the page shows *Your configurations notify nobody yet*. Its **Create the rule** button opens the rule form with the category, the event and a name already filled in.

## Monitor a Security Engine in one click

The quickest way to start is from the engines you already run. Editors and owners find it in three places:

- the banner *Is your host attacking?* at the top of a Security Engine's page, and the **Monitor with Am I Attacking** entry of its menu;
- the empty state of the Am I Attacking page, which lists up to three of your engines with a **Monitor** button.

The modal is prefilled with the engine's IP, which you can complete with other addresses, and picks the destination of the alerts from your active integrations, in this order: Email, Slack, Discord, webhook. With no integration linked yet, it offers to connect one first.

**Start monitoring** does two things in one go: it adds the IP to a configuration named **My Engines**, created on the fly with a one-hour deduplication window if it does not exist yet, and it creates a notification rule named *Am I Attacking - \<engine name\>* if none carries the event yet, or adds you to the email recipients of the existing rule. If the rule cannot be created, the configuration change is reverted so nothing is left half done.

## Read the events

The Am I Attacking page is the history of every event recorded for your organization.

<ThemedImage
    alt="The Am I Attacking page: 30-day activity, filters, the list of events and the details of the selected one"
    sources={{
        light: useBaseUrl("/img/console/am_i_attacking/events-light.png"),
        dark: useBaseUrl("/img/console/am_i_attacking/events-dark.png"),
    }}
/>

**Activity.** A heatmap sums the **last 30 days**, one square per UTC day, next to three counters: events, IPs involved and distinct scenarios. On an organization with a very large number of events, the summary stops reading after a point: the counters then carry a `+` suffix and read as a floor.

**The list.** Events are grouped by day, most recent first, 25 per page, with times in UTC. Each row shows the confidence of the detecting scenario, the source IP, the first behavior, the scenario, the configuration the IP belongs to, and the time.

**The details.** Select a row to see the behaviors with their descriptions, the **targeted range** (the attacked IP, truncated to its /24), the scenario and its confidence, the attack window, when it was detected, the configuration, and whether the decision was taken manually by the reporting engine. **Open in CTI** opens the source IP's [IP report](/u/console/ip_reputation/ip_report) in a new tab.

Confidence reads as **Low** below 3, **Medium** from 3, **High** from 5. It tells how reliably the scenario tells a real attack from legitimate traffic: a low confidence leaves room for a false positive. An event can outlive its configuration, or come from a source the Console cannot trace back to one. It then reads as *Not linked to any of your configurations*.

**Filters.** Narrow the list by source IP, configuration, minimum confidence and period (last 24 hours, 7 days, 30 days, or any time). **More filters** adds the scenario, the behavior and the targeted range. **Clear** removes them all.

## Manage configurations

**Configurations** in the page header opens the list. Each line shows the name, the entries and the number of addresses they cover, the deduplication window, and a status: **Attack seen** with the time of the last event when one happened in the last 7 days, **Quiet this week** otherwise.

Editors and owners can **edit** a configuration, which asks the declaration again when addresses come in, or **delete** it. Deleting stops the monitoring of its addresses at once; past events stay in the history.

## Activity log

Every change is recorded in the organization's **Activity** page, under three entries: configuration created, updated and deleted. Each one names who did it, the configuration, the addresses brought under monitoring or removed (the first ten listed, each linking to its IP report), the number of addresses now monitored, and any change of the deduplication window. When addresses were added, the entry also states that the author declared being authorized to monitor them, and on which date. Purchases, decreases and scheduled decreases of monitored IP slots are logged there too.

## Key considerations

- **Nothing is sent without a rule.** Events are always recorded, but a notification only goes out through a notification rule carrying the *Am I Attacking* event.
- **The declaration is a record.** Each acceptance is kept with your account email, the addresses, the exact wording and its date, and it survives the deletion of the configuration, of your account and of the organization.
- **An edit replaces the configuration.** The addresses and the window you save are the whole configuration; the Monitor Engine modal merges new addresses into *My Engines* for you.
- **Documentation and private ranges cannot be monitored.** The RFC 5737 blocks are refused like any reserved range.
- **Deduplication is per IP**, within a configuration: two of your IPs attacking at the same time produce two notifications.
