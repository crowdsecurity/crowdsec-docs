Your Security Engines tell you who attacks you. Am I Attacking tells you when the attacker is **one of your own IPs**: a compromised server, a hijacked workstation, a misconfigured scanner. You declare the addresses and ranges you operate, and every attack reported against them by the CrowdSec network lands in the Console, in your notification rules, and in your inbox or chat tools.

## How it works

1. **Declare the IPs you operate** in a *configuration*: single addresses, CIDR ranges, or both.
2. **The whole network watches them.** Every CrowdSec Security Engine that blocks an attack reports its source. When a source matches one of your addresses, an *Am I Attacking event* is recorded for your organization.
3. **You are alerted where you work.** A [notification rule](/u/console/notification_integrations/rule) carrying the *Am I Attacking* event delivers each event by Email, Slack, Discord or webhook.

:::info A configuration does not alert on its own
Declaring addresses records the events in the Console, but nothing leaves it until a notification rule carries the *Am I Attacking* event. The page warns you as long as no rule does, and offers to create one.
:::

## Who can see it

The page lives in the **IP Reputation / CTI** section of the Console, at [app.crowdsec.net/am-i-attacking](https://app.crowdsec.net/am-i-attacking).

| You are | What you get |
|---|---|
| Not signed in | A public presentation of the feature, with a link to create an account or sign in. |
| On a Community plan | A presentation of what Premium unlocks, with sample events and an upgrade button. The IP search page of the CTI also shows a dismissable banner pointing to the feature. |
| On a Premium or Enterprise plan | The feature itself: configurations, event history and quota. Editors and owners can create and change configurations; viewers can read everything. |

The command palette opens the page for every signed-in user. On a Premium organization it also offers *Add an Am I Attacking configuration* and *Am I Attacking configurations*.

## Declare the IPs to monitor

Click **Configurations** in the page header, then **Add a configuration**. A configuration carries a name, the addresses to watch and a deduplication window.

### IPs and CIDR ranges

Type an address or a range and press **Enter** after each one: every entry becomes a chip. You can also paste a whole list, separated by spaces, commas, semicolons or line breaks. When you focus the field, the IPs of the Security Engines you run are suggested so you can add them in a click.

Entries are checked as you type. The Console refuses, with an explanation:

- anything that is **not a valid IP or CIDR range**;
- **private or reserved** addresses, including loopback, link-local, multicast, and the documentation and benchmarking blocks (RFC 1918, 6598, 5737, 2544, 3849, 4193, among others). Any range overlapping one of them is refused as a whole;
- ranges that are a **/16 or wider** in IPv4, or a **/112 or wider** in IPv6. Monitoring such a range goes through our sales team: a *Contact us* link appears under the field.

IPv4 and IPv6 are both accepted, in the same configuration.

### How many IPs a range counts for

As soon as the configuration holds a range, a summary tells you how many addresses it monitors in all: a /24 counts for 256 addresses, a /32 for one. Each address is counted once. A single IP already inside one of your ranges, or a range nested in a wider one, is marked *already inside* and adds nothing.

Below the summary, a notice shows how many of your organization's monitored IPs this configuration will use after saving, against your [quota](#monitored-ip-quota).

### Deduplication window

After an alert on one of your IPs, further detections on **that same IP** stay silent for the length of the window. Pick a preset (15 minutes, 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours), or choose **Custom** and set days, hours and minutes. Set every unit to zero to be notified on every detection.

The default window is one hour.

### Declaration of authorization

Before saving, you must tick the declaration that you are authorized to monitor these addresses. CrowdSec records it with your account, the addresses declared, the wording you agreed to and the date.

The declaration is asked **every time addresses are added**, on creation and on edition. Renaming a configuration, changing its window or removing addresses asks for nothing. When a CrowdSec support member acts on your behalf, the record names them as the one who declared, and you as the member the addresses were declared for.

## Monitored IP quota

A Premium plan includes **3 monitored IPs**, counted across all your configurations. The gauge in the page header shows how many are used, how many are left, and lets the organization owner add more. The same gauge sits on the **Plan** page of the organization settings, under *Am I Attacking monitored IPs*.

### Adding monitored IP slots

When a configuration would exceed the limit, the modal says by how many addresses and quotes the monthly price of the missing slots. The button becomes **Buy N slots and create configuration** (or *save changes*): the slots are added to the subscription first, then the configuration is saved. Expect a few seconds between the two while the new limit propagates; if it takes longer, the slots are bought and the Console asks you to save again in a minute.

Only an **organization owner**, on an **active Premium subscription**, can buy slots. Editors see the shortfall and a message saying who can fix it.

Slots can also be added or removed from the Plan page:

- an **increase** applies immediately, prorated over the current billing period;
- a **decrease** is scheduled for the end of the billing period, and refused if more addresses are monitored than the new limit would allow. A decrease already scheduled is cancelled if you monitor more addresses than it leaves room for in the meantime.

A Premium subscription can monitor up to **16,384 addresses** in all, the equivalent of a /18. Beyond that, the modal invites you to contact us.

### During the free trial

Monitoring more than **16 IPs** is not covered by the free trial. Buying slots past that number ends the trial at once, and billing starts immediately. The modal warns you before you confirm.

## Get notified

Events reach you through your [notification rules](/u/console/notification_integrations/rule). Create a rule in the **Threat Hunting** category, select the **Am I Attacking** event, and pick any destination: Email, Slack, Discord or a webhook. The deduplication window of each configuration applies to every destination.

As long as no rule carries the event and reaches an active integration, the Am I Attacking page shows a warning: *Your configurations notify nobody yet*. Its **Create the rule** button opens the rule form with the category, the event and a name already filled in.

## Monitor a Security Engine in one click

The quickest way to start is from the engines you already run. Three places offer it, to editors and owners of the organization that owns the engine:

- the banner *Is your host attacking?* at the top of a Security Engine's page, and the **Monitor with Am I Attacking** entry of its menu;
- the empty state of the Am I Attacking page, which lists up to three of your engines with a **Monitor** button.

The modal is prefilled with the engine's IP, which you can complete with other addresses. It also picks the destination of the alerts from your active integrations, in this order: Email, Slack, Discord, webhook. With no integration linked yet, the modal offers to connect one first.

Confirming **Start monitoring** does two things in one go:

1. it adds the IP to a configuration named **My Engines**, created on the fly with a one-hour deduplication window if it does not exist yet;
2. it creates a notification rule named *Am I Attacking - \<engine name\>* if none carries the event yet, or adds you to the email recipients of the existing rule when you are not among them.

If the rule cannot be created, the configuration change is reverted so nothing is left half done. Should the reversal fail too, the Console says so and sends you to the rule form to finish by hand.

Once monitored, the engine carries a green **Monitored** tag on its page and in its menu. Opening the modal again shows which rule alerts you, and lets you **Notify me too**, **Stop my alerts** (which removes you from the rule, or deletes it when it only notified you), or **Create the rule** when none exists.

## Read the events

The Am I Attacking page is the history of every event recorded for your organization.

### Activity

A heatmap sums the **last 30 days**, one square per UTC day, next to three counters: events, IPs involved and distinct scenarios. On an organization with a very large number of events, the summary stops reading after a point: the counters then show a `+` suffix and read as a floor.

### The list

Events are grouped by day, most recent first, 25 per page, with times in UTC. Each row shows the confidence of the detecting scenario, the source IP, the first behavior, the scenario, the configuration the IP belongs to, and the time.

Select a row to open its details: the behaviors with their descriptions, the **targeted range** (the attacked IP, truncated to its /24), the scenario, its confidence, the attack window, when it was detected, the configuration, and whether the decision was taken manually by the reporting engine. **Open in CTI** opens the source IP's [IP report](/u/console/ip_reputation/ip_report) in a new tab, and a copy button puts the address in your clipboard.

Confidence reads as **Low** below 3, **Medium** from 3, **High** from 5. It tells how reliably the scenario tells a real attack from legitimate traffic: a low confidence leaves room for a false positive.

An event can outlive its configuration, or come from a source the Console cannot trace back to one. It then reads as *Not linked to any of your configurations*.

### Filters

Filter the list by **source IP**, **configuration**, **minimum confidence** and **period** (last 24 hours, 7 days, 30 days, or any time). **More filters** adds the scenario, the behavior and the targeted range. Text filters apply when you press Enter or leave the field; dropdowns apply at once. **Clear** removes them all.

### Empty states

- With no configuration yet, the page suggests the Security Engines you run, or walks you through the first declaration.
- With configurations and no event, the page confirms that monitoring is active, how many IPs are watched, and whether a rule delivers the alerts. No event is good news.

## Manage configurations

**Configurations** in the page header opens the list. Each line shows the name, the entries and the number of addresses they cover, the deduplication window, and a status: **Attack seen** with the time of the last event when one happened in the last 7 days, **Quiet this week** otherwise.

Editors and owners can **edit** a configuration (which asks the declaration again when addresses come in) or **delete** it. Deleting stops the monitoring of its addresses at once; past events stay in the history.

## Activity log

Every change is recorded in the organization's **Activity** page, under three entries: configuration created, updated and deleted. Each one names who did it, the configuration, the addresses brought under monitoring or removed (the first ten listed, each linking to its IP report), the number of addresses now monitored, and any change of the deduplication window. When addresses were added, the entry also states that the author declared being authorized to monitor them, and on which date.

Purchases, decreases and scheduled decreases of monitored IP slots are logged there too.

## Key considerations

- **Nothing is sent without a rule.** Events are always recorded, but a notification only goes out through a notification rule carrying the *Am I Attacking* event.
- **The declaration is a record.** Each acceptance is kept with your account email, the addresses, the exact wording and its date, and it survives the deletion of the configuration, of your account and of the organization.
- **An edit replaces the configuration.** The addresses and the window you save are the whole configuration; the Monitor Engine modal merges new addresses into *My Engines* for you.
- **Documentation and private ranges cannot be monitored.** The addresses used as examples in this documentation, drawn from the RFC 5737 blocks, would be refused by the Console like any reserved range.
- **IPv4 and IPv6 count alike** against the quota.
- **Deduplication is per IP**, within a configuration: two of your IPs attacking at the same time produce two notifications.
- **The quota is enforced server-side.** The Console anticipates it, but the API has the last word and the Console reports its refusal.
- **In data anonymisation mode**, IPs, ranges, configuration names and engine names are masked on every Am I Attacking screen, and the copy button of an event is hidden.
